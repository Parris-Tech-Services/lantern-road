# Player-Leader Party Contract

Owner: **Agent 7 — The Director**  
Task: **LR-0089**  
Final creative authority: **Josh**

This is the canonical product contract for who the player is in Lantern Road and where companion autonomy stops.

## Core party rule

The active adventuring party is **four people total**:

1. **the player Leader**;
2. **three active companions**.

Garrick Vale, Mira Quickstep, Oren Thale and Sister Brindle all remain authored recruitable companions.

When all four companions are available, one is in **reserve** rather than being deleted, rewritten into the Leader, or discarded from the campaign.

The intended feeling is:

> **You are in the party. You lead people, not pieces.**

The current prototype's four-companion roster is transitional implementation, not the long-term fictional model.

## Companion roster and reserve

The **companion roster** is the set of authored companions currently available to travel with the Leader.

The **active companions** are the three currently travelling with the Leader.

A **reserve companion** remains a persistent character with:

- Trust and relationship history;
- personal-arc state;
- injuries/conditions where relevant;
- equipment/state that belongs to that character;
- remembered conversations they personally experienced;
- future availability unless story consequences legitimately change it.

Reserve does not mean erased from the story.

### Rotation guardrails

Roster rotation should happen at a believable safe transition, such as camp or a settlement, not as a free mid-combat swap.

A reserve companion must not magically remember events they did not witness. Shared party news may later be conveyed through authored dialogue, but direct memories/interjections require plausible presence or explicit later telling.

Do not use rotation as a punishment mechanic that forces constant roster micromanagement.

## Minimal Leader identity

Lantern Road needs enough Leader identity for NPCs and companions to address and remember the player without turning the game into a large character creator.

Canonical minimum:

- **Leader name** — player-chosen display name; a neutral fallback such as “Leader” may exist until one is entered.
- **At most one explicit background tag** — optional/light-touch context for a small number of authored lines or checks. The exact small option set belongs to later authoring/UX work.
- **Emergent leadership memories** — remembered behaviour such as keeping promises, asking for advice, commanding frequently, protecting secrets or accepting correction.

The emergent memories are not a visible morality/alignment system.

Do **not** require, purely for this model:

- gender;
- physical appearance;
- age;
- voice;
- class;
- religion;
- birthplace;
- romance history;
- a long skill-tree character creator.

Those can only be added through an explicit future product decision.

## Speaker and decision authority

The Leader is the **default speaker** in external conversations.

NPCs primarily address the Leader. Companions may interject, advise, disagree and be directly addressed.

The Leader owns the final decision on **party-level irreversible choices**, including:

- accepting or rejecting major quests on behalf of the party;
- choosing a core quest resolution;
- making/breaking party-level promises;
- revealing or withholding major party-held information;
- committing unique/rare resources where the spend cannot reasonably be undone;
- choosing a faction/campaign commitment that closes major branches;
- initiating an irreversible betrayal, execution or equivalent high-consequence act.

A companion may argue strongly, refuse personal participation where appropriate, or change the social cost of the choice. They do not silently replace the player's decision.

## Companion agency

Companions are **persuadable, not programmable**.

They may, when consistent with established characterisation and current circumstances:

- advise;
- interject;
- hesitate;
- question the Leader;
- accept reluctantly;
- refuse a personal request;
- counter-offer;
- volunteer;
- disclose or withhold a personal matter;
- object to the Leader's wording or reasoning;
- react differently based on Trust, relationships, fatigue, injury, promises and prior treatment;
- take a bounded proactive action when it is clearly telegraphed and does not steal an irreversible party decision.

This agency should make them feel like people, not introduce random obstruction.

## What companions may refuse

A companion can refuse or renegotiate requests that primarily concern **their own body, conscience, identity or personal boundary**.

Examples:

- accepting treatment;
- revealing a personal secret;
- lending their authority/faith/name to a claim;
- taking a dangerous role;
- destroying evidence they personally consider morally binding;
- making a statement they believe is false.

High Trust increases honesty, negotiation and benefit of the doubt. It is not obedience.

## Commands

Commands remain part of leadership.

They are most legitimate in:

- immediate danger;
- time-critical coordination;
- combat;
- an explicitly assumed command responsibility.

A command can produce:

- willing compliance;
- reluctant compliance;
- a social cost;
- a counterproposal;
- refusal when the request crosses a strong personal/value boundary.

Repeated use of authority where cooperation was available should be rememberable.

## Bounded proactive behaviour

A companion may sometimes act proactively because of a well-established trait, but only inside clear limits.

Good examples:

- a bold companion volunteers to take point;
- a protective companion moves to cover someone in immediate danger;
- a guarded companion declines to volunteer personal information;
- a principled companion corrects a false claim in conversation;
- an injured/proud companion initially refuses treatment.

Proactive behaviour must be:

1. **character-grounded** rather than random;
2. **readable/telegraphed** where the player could reasonably anticipate it;
3. **bounded** in consequence;
4. **explainable afterward** through dialogue or visible state;
5. **rare enough** that leadership still feels meaningful.

## Things companions do not take away silently

Without an explicit authored crisis exception, a companion must not autonomously:

- choose a core quest/faction ending for the player;
- spend a unique or scarce campaign resource;
- sell/discard a unique item;
- permanently dismiss another companion;
- make a binding party promise;
- disclose a protected party secret with major campaign consequence;
- start an avoidable major battle that closes peaceful options;
- override the Leader's final decision merely because an AI/personality roll fired.

If a companion is about to create a major irreversible consequence, surface the disagreement and let the Leader respond unless the entire dramatic point is an explicitly authored, previously telegraphed character break.

## Refusal ladder

Use the lightest response that preserves character integrity:

1. **advice** — “I think we should…”
2. **hesitation** — “Are you sure?”
3. **counter-offer** — “I'll do it if…”
4. **reluctant acceptance** — complies but records disagreement;
5. **soft refusal** — can be discussed;
6. **value refusal** — clear personal boundary;
7. **crisis refusal/proactive break** — rare, heavily authored, strongly telegraphed.

Hard refusal should not be a routine dice result.

## Player-control test

Before giving a companion autonomous behaviour, ask:

- Does this reveal character or merely obstruct the player?
- Could the player reasonably understand why it happened?
- Does it preserve the Leader's authority over irreversible party choices?
- Is the consequence proportional to how strongly the behaviour was telegraphed?
- Can the player respond socially or tactically afterward?
- Would repeating this often make the player feel they are not actually leading?

If the last answer is yes, reduce the autonomy.
