# Consequential Dialogue Choice Grammar

## Principle

A dialogue option is worthwhile when changing it would change one of:

- information;
- relationship;
- commitment;
- future availability;
- remembered tone;
- immediate action;
- interpretation of the same action.

If three choices all produce the same paragraph and same consequences, they are not three choices.

---

# Choice intents

## ASK

Purpose: learn.

Examples:

> “What happened?”

> “Who else knows?”

> “Why do you care where the ledger ends up?”

Possible consequence:

- unlocks information;
- signals interest;
- may expose that the Leader already knows more than they should.

ASK should not disappear after use. On repeat, it becomes:

> “You said the ledger changed hands at the ferry. Anything else?”

not the original question again.

---

## PROBE

Purpose: challenge an incomplete answer.

Examples:

> “That isn’t what I asked.”

> “You’re protecting someone.”

> “You said three wagons. Tessa said one.”

PROBE may:

- increase respect with direct characters;
- reduce Trust with guarded characters;
- reveal optional truth;
- close a topic if pushed too hard.

---

## REASSURE

Purpose: reduce fear or signal solidarity.

Examples:

> “You don’t have to explain it tonight.”

> “I’m not handing you over.”

> “We’ll make a decision together.”

REASSURE should not be free approval.

Mira may distrust reassurance that sounds like a promise the Leader cannot guarantee.

---

## CHALLENGE

Purpose: confront values or behaviour.

Examples:

> “You keep calling this duty. I think you’re afraid to let anyone help.”

> “If the Archive owns the truth, it can bury it politely.”

> “Mercy still chooses who waits.”

CHALLENGE is often the right path to deeper relationships, not automatically a negative option.

High Trust should make difficult honesty more possible.

---

## SUPPORT

Purpose: affirm a character's judgement/value.

Example:

> “You were right to keep the route secret.”

This can strengthen Trust **and** reinforce a flaw.

Approval should not always equal healthy growth.

---

## JOKE

Purpose: release tension, test intimacy, deflect.

A joke should fit the speaker and moment.

It may:

- strengthen Trust at high familiarity;
- be poorly received after recent harm;
- let the Leader avoid answering;
- create a recurring private joke.

Avoid generic quips in every scene.

---

## PROMISE

Purpose: create future obligation.

Always define:

- what was promised;
- to whom;
- what would count as kept/broken/renegotiated;
- at least one later callback point.

---

## LIE

Purpose: deliberately establish a false belief.

Always define:

- who knows it is false;
- who believes it;
- what evidence could expose it;
- what later relationship risk exists.

Do not use lies as flavour with no memory.

---

## EVADE

Purpose: avoid answering without lying.

Example:

> “What matters is where the medicine goes now.”

Useful with political NPCs.

Some characters respect boundaries.

Others read evasion as distrust.

---

## REFUSE

Purpose: reject request, premise or authority.

Refusal should often keep conversation alive.

Example:

> “No. I’ll hear Nera before I give you the ledger.”

This is more interesting than closing dialogue.

---

## DEFER

Purpose: give another person authority.

Examples:

> “Mira, you know these routes. Your call.”

> “Brindle, tell me who needs this first.”

Later callback should remember that the Leader trusted them with responsibility.

---

## COMMAND

Purpose: assert leadership.

Use sparingly.

A command may:

- force immediate action if plausible;
- cost Trust;
- gain respect from a character who values decisiveness;
- be refused at low Trust or severe value conflict;
- trigger another member to intervene.

---

## ADMIT UNCERTAINTY

Purpose: be honest about limits.

Examples:

> “I don’t know.”

> “I was sure this morning. I’m not now.”

> “We need more information.”

This can be strong leadership.

It can also frustrate someone in an emergency.

---

# Dialogue option presentation

## Label should carry intent

Bad:

> Ask about the ledger.

Better:

> “Who gets hurt if this ledger becomes public?”

Better choices reduce the gap between button and spoken line.

## Avoid fake paraphrase choices

Bad:

1. “I agree.”
2. “You’re right.”
3. “That makes sense.”

Unless tone itself is meaningfully remembered, this is one option.

## Allow silence when silence is authored

Silence can be an actual choice.

Example:

Brindle cannot finish a prayer.

> Sit beside her.

That is not “skip dialogue.”

It is a social action.

# Conversation depth tiers

Not every interaction needs a giant tree.

## Tier 1 — Functional exchange

Use for:

- shop/service explanation;
- small rumour;
- immediate road information.

2–4 meaningful options.

## Tier 2 — Relationship conversation

Use for:

- recurring named NPC;
- camp conversation;
- follow-up after important choice.

Usually:

- opening;
- 2–4 topics;
- at least one remembered branch;
- one optional deeper branch.

## Tier 3 — Major decision conversation

Use for:

- core quest resolution;
- personal quest;
- campaign faction pressure;
- final convergence.

Should include:

- competing arguments;
- ability to ask questions before committing;
- party interjections;
- refusal/delay where plausible;
- final choice separated from information-gathering choices.

The player should not be forced to decide before they have been allowed to understand.

---

# Consequence layers

A major dialogue choice may create consequences at multiple timescales.

## Immediate

- reaction line;
- Trust change;
- another member objects;
- NPC changes tone;
- action happens.

## Short delayed

Later that day/camp:

> “You made me part of that lie.”

## Medium delayed

Return to settlement:

> “Last time you promised…”

## Campaign delayed

Faction pressure:

> “You already proved whose word you trust.”

## Ending

Epilogue remembers a defining promise, betrayal, act of trust or unresolved disagreement.

Not every choice needs all four layers.

Important choices should rarely have only the immediate layer.

# Memory states for topics

Each important topic should conceptually support:

- `unheard`
- `heard`
- `pressed`
- `resolved`
- `closed`
- optional `changed` after new world information

Player-facing text never exposes these names.

Example: Mira old-name topic.

### Unheard

> “Why did you erase that road?”

### Heard

> “You said people there knew you by another name.”

### Pressed

> “You still haven’t told me what the name was.”

### Closed

If player promised not to press:

> “I said I wouldn’t ask again.”

### Changed

After someone calls her Venn:

> “Was that the name you meant?”

The game should never restart at:

> “Why did you erase that road?”

---

# Information gating

A dialogue choice can depend on what the Leader knows.

If the player found the ledger first, do not ask Oswin:

> “What ledger?”

Offer:

> “I found your ledger.”

or:

> “Before I tell you what I found, tell me what is in it.”

This is a simple but powerful form of responsiveness.

---

# No exhaust-every-topic design

Avoid making every NPC feel like a wiki where the optimal play is clicking every line from top to bottom.

Some topics should be:

- mutually exclusive by tone;
- unavailable after a lie;
- opened by Trust;
- made irrelevant by later events;
- revisited with changed meaning.

A conversation should feel like a social moment, not 100% completion UI.
