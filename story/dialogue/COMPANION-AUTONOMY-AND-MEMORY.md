# Companion Autonomy & Conversation Memory

## Shared rule

Garrick, Mira, Oren and Brindle should be **persuadable, not programmable**.

Their response to the Leader depends on:

- Trust;
- prior memories;
- relationship with other party members;
- personal values;
- fatigue/injury when relevant;
- what the Leader is asking;
- whether the request contradicts something the Leader previously promised;
- urgency.

---

# Garrick Vale

## Social pattern

Garrick usually complies with practical requests.

He resists:

- being ordered to rest when others are exposed;
- plans that shift danger to someone he considers less able to bear it;
- evasive leadership in immediate danger.

He respects:

- shared burden;
- clear responsibility;
- Leader taking risk personally;
- direct admission of cost.

## Suggestion example

Leader:

> “Garrick, take a bandage before we move.”

### High Trust, lightly hurt

> “All right.”
>
> He takes it before he can turn accepting help into an argument.

### Low Trust

> “Save it.”
>
> “I’m moving fine.”

Leader may then:

- accept refusal;
- explain why;
- command;
- offer to use one too.

### Seriously hurt

Even high Trust may not make him eager.

> “If I use it, we have one less for whoever drops next.”

His refusal comes from values, not random stubbornness.

## Delayed callback

If the Leader repeatedly shares burden:

> At the next camp, Garrick hands you the watch slate.
>
> “I put my name in once.”
>
> “Don't make a ceremony of it.”

If the Leader repeatedly commands him to take risk:

> “You don't have to convince me to stand in front.”
>
> “That is exactly why you should think before you ask.”

# Mira Quickstep

## Social pattern

Mira complies when she feels she still owns the decision.

She resists:

- orders that expose her history;
- institutional obedience for its own sake;
- promises made on her behalf;
- being asked to trust someone without information.

She respects:

- honesty;
- room to manoeuvre;
- Leader admitting uncertainty;
- being trusted to read people/routes.

## Suggestion example

Leader:

> “Mira, talk to Nera. She’ll hear it better from you.”

### High Trust

> “Probably.”
>
> “Do I get to choose what I say?”
>
> Leader:
>
> - “Yes. I trust you.”
> - “Stay close to what we agreed.”
> - “No. I need one message delivered exactly.”

Each produces different tone.

### Low Trust

> “Interesting.”
>
> “You want my face attached to your decision.”

She may still do it if the purpose aligns with her values.

## Delayed callback

If the Leader trusted her wording:

> “Nera asked whether you knew what I was going to say.”
>
> Mira smiles.
>
> “I told her that was the point.”

If the Leader micromanaged her:

> “Next time you want your exact words spoken, teach Garrick ventriloquism.”

# Oren Thale

## Social pattern

Oren often agrees if given a reason.

He resists:

- destroying evidence;
- certainty unsupported by facts;
- treating expertise as obedience;
- dismissing inconvenient information because it complicates the plan.

He respects:

- explicit reasoning;
- corrections;
- Leader changing their mind after new evidence;
- being asked to explain rather than merely validate.

## Suggestion example

Leader:

> “Oren, tell Crow the chart proves this route is safe.”

### If chart only proves historical use

He refuses the wording.

> “No.”
>
> “It proves the route existed.”
>
> “If you want me to say safe, first provide evidence that the river still respects ninety-year-old cartography.”

Leader may:

- accept correction;
- order him to simplify;
- ask for safer wording;
- deliver claim personally.

## Delayed callback

If Leader accepted correction:

> Oren later hands over a revised note.
>
> “Thank you for letting the evidence ruin a convenient sentence.”

If Leader forced the claim:

> “It turned out to be safe.”
>
> “That does not make what we said true when we said it.”

# Sister Brindle

## Social pattern

Brindle often helps when asked.

She resists:

- cruelty presented as efficiency;
- using faith to manipulate frightened people;
- pretending a painful choice is morally clean;
- promises of safety nobody can guarantee.

She respects:

- mercy that names its cost;
- Leader sharing responsibility;
- silence when words would only serve the speaker;
- willingness to face people harmed by a decision.

## Suggestion example

Leader:

> “Brindle, tell them the Lamp protects this road.”

She may refuse regardless of Trust.

> “I can tell them I’ll walk it with them.”
>
> “I cannot tell them the Lamp signed a patrol schedule.”

The refusal should feel like character integrity, not a failed command mechanic.

## Delayed callback

If Leader accepts her boundary:

> Later, Brindle says:
>
> “Thank you for letting me offer comfort without making me sell certainty.”

If Leader repeatedly uses her authority instrumentally:

> “Please stop asking me to make your decision sound holy.”

That is a relationship rupture worth feeling.

# Refusal ladder

Companion refusal should have degrees.

## Hesitation

> “Are you sure?”

The Leader can explain.

## Counterproposal

> “I'll do it if we also…”

## Soft refusal

> “I'd rather not.”

Trust may allow discussion.

## Value refusal

> “No.”

High Trust does not erase this.

## Crisis refusal

Rare.

A party member may refuse because:

- action violates core value;
- relationship is severely damaged;
- they are physically incapable;
- Leader asks them to betray a specific promise/memory.

The game should not overuse hard refusal or the player stops feeling like Leader.

---

# Trust is not obedience

High Trust means:

- harder conversations are possible;
- party member gives the Leader more benefit of the doubt;
- private topics open;
- criticism may become more direct because the relationship can survive it.

High Trust does **not** mean:

- automatic agreement;
- values disappear;
- commands never cost anything.

A high-Trust refusal can be more intimate:

> “No.”
>
> Mira looks genuinely sorry.
>
> “And you know me well enough to know why.”

---

# Fatigue and injury

When later systems expose them, fatigue/injury should change social availability.

Examples:

- exhausted Mira becomes less playful and more blunt;
- hurt Garrick is more likely to minimise his condition;
- injured Oren may become irritable when treated as fragile;
- exhausted Brindle may fail to perform emotional labour for everyone else.

Do not make every condition produce generic:

> “I am tired.”

Use condition as pressure on established personality.

# Conversation memory rules

## 1. First meeting happens once

After Rowan has introduced the old-road problem, he should never again speak as though the Leader has never heard of it.

## 2. Asked questions become references

First:

> “Why do you need the ledger?”

Repeat:

> “You said the ledger could hurt the Guild. How?”

Later changed state:

> “Now that I’ve seen the ledger, you left something out.”

## 3. Promises stay visible

A promise should be referenceable until:

- kept;
- broken;
- explicitly released by the person;
- made impossible by changed circumstances.

## 4. Refused topics can cool down

If Mira says:

> “Not tonight.”

The option should not immediately reappear unchanged.

Later:

> “You said not tonight. Is tonight different?”

## 5. New evidence reopens old topics

Conversation memory should not mean topics are permanently consumed.

Moonmere chart found:

> Oren, earlier you said there was no evidence of an older crossing.

Now:

> “I was wrong.”

That is the pleasure of a responsive world.

---

# Memory granularity

Do not remember every sentence forever.

Prioritise:

- promises;
- lies;
- major disagreement;
- deference/trust;
- personal boundaries;
- quest-resolution reasoning;
- personal-arc revelations;
- repeated leadership behaviour.

Minor flavour can fade.

---

# Repetition-safe dialogue

NPCs/companions need at least three modes where appropriate:

1. **first delivery**;
2. **short repeat**;
3. **changed-state callback**.

Example, Garrick bandage topic:

First:

> “Save it for someone who needs it.”

Repeat same state:

> “Still moving.”

Changed after worsening injury:

> Garrick looks at the bandage.
>
> “All right.”
>
> “Don't smile.”

---

# Party interjections

Major conversations should allow party members to interject when their values are directly implicated.

Interjections should:

- add perspective;
- expose disagreement;
- alter available Leader choices;
- sometimes make NPC react differently.

They should not replace the Leader's final agency unless the Leader explicitly defers.
