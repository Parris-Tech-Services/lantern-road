# The Player as Party Leader

## 1. Fictional role

The Leader is **present**.

NPCs address the Leader directly.

Party members can look to the Leader for a decision, challenge the Leader's wording, refuse a suggestion or remember something the Leader promised.

Narration may say:

> Rowan looks to you.

> Mira waits to see whether you are joking.

> Garrick does not move until you finish speaking.

Avoid narration that makes the player sound like an external operator:

> Select which party member performs the action.

Prefer:

> “Mira, can you read the tracks?”

This difference is small mechanically and enormous emotionally.

---

# 2. What leadership means

Leadership is **social authority**, not ownership.

The Leader can:

- **ask**;
- **suggest**;
- **request**;
- **promise**;
- **challenge**;
- **reassure**;
- **joke**;
- **lie**;
- **refuse**;
- **defer**;
- **command** when urgency plausibly permits it.

The party members are not puppets.

A command can succeed mechanically and still cost Trust.

A suggestion can be rejected.

A request can be renegotiated.

A party member can obey while making it clear they disagree.

The intended feeling is:

> **You lead people, not pieces.**

---

# 3. The Leader should not have a fixed morality

Do not offer only:

- kind answer;
- neutral answer;
- cruel answer.

That creates a morality menu instead of a role-playing conversation.

Dialogue choices should instead differ by **intent**.

Example: Garrick wants to take first watch while injured.

Weak choices:

1. Let him.
2. Don't let him.
3. Leave.

Better choices:

1. **“You’ve done enough. I’m taking the first watch.”**  
   Intent: protect Garrick; risk making him feel managed.

2. **“Take half a watch. Wake Mira after.”**  
   Intent: compromise; shares burden.

3. **“If you say you can do it, I trust you.”**  
   Intent: respect autonomy; may reinforce self-sacrifice.

4. **“No. That’s an order.”**  
   Intent: assert leadership; may protect him physically while costing Trust.

Each answer says something about the Leader.

---

# 4. Suggestion before command

Outside immediate combat danger, the default verb should be **suggest**, not command.

Examples:

> “Mira, could you take a look?”

> “Oren, what do you make of this?”

> “Brindle, can you talk to them?”

> “Garrick, hold here while we move the cart?”

This lets party personality matter.

The response may be:

- enthusiastic agreement;
- reluctant agreement;
- an alternative proposal;
- refusal;
- agreement with a condition;
- another party member objecting.

## Command is still meaningful

Commands belong in:

- immediate danger;
- time-critical action;
- moments where the Leader chooses authority over consensus;
- relationship scenes where asserting authority itself is the point.

Repeated commands should have a social cost if used where cooperation was available.

---

# 5. The Leader can admit uncertainty

Premium dialogue should not force the player to pretend expertise.

Useful choice patterns:

> “I don’t know yet.”

> “What would you do?”

> “I need another opinion.”

> “We don’t have enough information.”

> “I promised something I’m no longer sure we can keep.”

Admitting uncertainty can:

- increase Trust with a member who values honesty;
- decrease confidence with someone who needs decisive action;
- unlock a companion proposal;
- create a shared decision.

The game should not treat indecision and honesty as the same thing.

---

# 6. Promises are first-class dialogue choices

A promise is more interesting than a reputation point because it creates future tension.

Examples:

> “We’ll get the medicine there before nightfall.”

> “I won’t give the ledger to Oswin without hearing you out.”

> “We’ll come back for the people at Mosslight.”

> “I won’t ask you about the old name again.”

A promise should be recorded only when it is specific enough to break.

Later conversation can acknowledge:

- kept;
- broken;
- impossible to keep;
- renegotiated honestly;
- forgotten by the player but not by the character.

## Broken promises

Do not always punish with a numeric loss.

Sometimes the consequence is better as changed language.

Before:

> “You said we would.”

After:

> “Tell me what you *intend* to do. Leave promises out of it.”

That is social consequence the player can feel.

---

# 7. Lies

Lies should be authored deliberately, not as a universal “deception option.”

A lie is interesting when:

- it protects someone;
- it avoids conflict now;
- it creates a fact another person may later contradict;
- a party member knows it is a lie;
- the Leader is making a promise they may not keep.

Example with Crow:

> Crow: “Did Nera give you the route names?”

Possible Leader choices:

1. **“Yes.”** — truthful disclosure.
2. **“No.”** — direct lie; Mira knows.
3. **“She gave us enough to understand the problem.”** — evasive truth.
4. **“Ask her.”** — refusal to become the source.

The later consequence is not just “Guile check passed.”

Mira may later say:

> “You lied cleanly.”

or:

> “You made me part of a lie without asking.”

---

# 8. The Leader can defer

Sometimes the most in-character option is:

> “Mira, your call.”

> “Brindle, I want your judgement.”

> “Garrick, you know this kind of ground.”

Deference should not make the player disappear.

The Leader is choosing **who gets authority**.

The chosen party member may remember being trusted.

Another member may remember being ignored.

This is especially useful when the party disagrees.

---

# 9. Leadership under disagreement

When two members disagree, avoid simple popularity voting.

Example:

Garrick wants a direct route.

Mira wants a deceptive route.

The Leader may:

- back Garrick;
- back Mira;
- ask them to build a joint plan;
- ask Oren for a third analysis;
- postpone;
- make a different decision entirely.

A strong conversation creates **triangles**, not just Leader-to-companion approval.

---

# 10. The Leader should appear in consequences

After a major decision, party-member reactions should sometimes address the Leader's method, not only the outcome.

Example: medicine delivered on time.

Generic:

> Brindle is pleased.

Leader-aware:

> Brindle waits until Yor carries the crate inside.
>
> “You never promised we would make it.”
>
> She looks at you.
>
> “You just kept choosing the road that gave us a chance.”
>
> “I trusted that more.”

This remembers **how** the Leader led.

---

# 11. No forced self-insert biography

The game should let the player inhabit the Leader without answering questions they never asked.

Avoid casually establishing:

- birthplace;
- family;
- former military rank;
- religion;
- romantic history;
- age;
- class background;
- physical appearance.

Player-facing choices may imply personality without fixing biography.

Good:

> “I grew up around roads like this.”  
Only offer this if the game explicitly supports background selection.

Until then:

> “I’ve seen roads fail faster than people expect.”

The second line expresses perspective without inventing childhood canon.

---

# 12. Leader identity can emerge through remembered style

Without a morality meter, the game can still remember patterns.

Possible authored tendencies for later implementation:

- keeps promises;
- uses commands often;
- asks companions for judgement;
- lies to institutions;
- prioritises vulnerable people;
- prioritises reliability;
- avoids violence;
- chooses directness;
- protects secrets.

Do not show these as alignment labels.

They exist to vary callbacks.

NPC:

> “You usually ask before you decide.”

is better than:

> Diplomatic Leader +3.
