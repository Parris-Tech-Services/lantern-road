# Regional Ending Prose

# The Gilded Road

## High legitimacy

The first new mile-post goes up before the mud has dried.

It carries a Gilt Caravan Guild mark, a maintenance date, a toll schedule and, beneath all three, the limits the party insisted upon at Watcher's Rest.

Wagons begin moving in longer strings.

Greyfen likes the predictability. Hearthwick likes seeing shelves fill before Tessa starts cutting onions thinner. Alderwatch likes deliveries that arrive on a day Yor Dane can write down in advance.

The improvement is real.

So is the new arithmetic.

A route with steady freight gets stone first. A crossing with too little traffic waits longer. Places that cannot make their need look like volume learn to argue in numbers the Guild understands.

Oswin Marris calls it responsibility.

Nera Vale calls it ownership with better stationery.

Both are being unfair.

Neither is entirely wrong.

The party's name matters enough that the Guild cannot pretend the settlement was unconditional. Complaints from smaller routes reach meetings they would once have missed. Some are answered.

Some are priced.

By winter, the road is easier to plan.

It is also easier to price than it is to argue with.

## Low legitimacy

The Guild does not need the party's permission.

It needs the same thing everyone else needs: roads that move.

So Greyfen's factors standardise tolls, schedule repairs and concentrate wagons along routes they can maintain predictably.

Trade improves.

That should not be dismissed simply because the party did not write the agreement.

Hearthwick sees more carts. Alderwatch sees fewer missed deliveries. Vesk gets clearer crossing schedules.

The quieter consequence arrives later.

Routes without enough freight start disappearing from ordinary conversation.

Not closed.

Not forbidden.

Simply not repaired soon enough to remain convenient.

The party's objections survive in a few local concessions and in the memories of people who heard them.

The Gilded Road is not cruel.

It simply knows how to count.

# The Guarded Road

## High legitimacy

New lanterns appear beside the road.

Some mark safe crossings.

Some mark inspection points.

It takes only a few days before travellers learn the difference by colour.

Edda Briar receives broader emergency authority, but not the blank authority several Wardens asked for.

Closure orders have to be recorded. Seized medicine has to be accounted for. Temporary checkpoints have dates attached.

Those limits exist because the party had enough standing to make “temporary” answer a second question:

> Until when?

The main routes become safer.

Bandit crews move farther out. Ferries receive patrol support. When flood water rises, somebody has the authority to close one crossing before three carts discover the same danger separately.

The cost is also visible.

People who once travelled without explaining themselves now meet ropes, lanterns and questions.

Mira calls the first checkpoint a road with a door.

Garrick calls it a door with somebody responsible for opening it.

Neither description wins.

At dusk, more lanterns burn beside the road.

Not all of them are there to welcome travellers.

## Low legitimacy

The Wardens act because the season is hardening and somebody has to act.

They establish patrol priorities, closures and checkpoints along the routes they can still hold.

Safety improves unevenly.

The party's name opens some conversations, not the policy itself.

Edda listens when they object to a specific closure. Crow remembers when they were right before.

That is enough to change several local decisions.

It is not enough to redesign emergency authority while everyone is already using it.

People sleep easier near the main roads.

People who rely on unofficial crossings learn new patrol times.

The Guarded Road works.

It also asks more travellers to explain why they are travelling.

# The Recorded Road

## High legitimacy

The new map is posted where travellers can touch it.

That detail matters to Sen Marrow.

It matters even more to the people who immediately begin writing corrections in the margins.

Candlemere publishes route obligations, closure rules, disputed tolls and the evidence supporting them.

The Gilt Caravan Guild hates some entries.

The Wardens hate several footnotes.

The Ashen Veil dislikes the entire concept of a public appendix.

They all keep reading.

The party's standing is strong enough that the Archive cannot make preservation synonymous with control.

Local testimony becomes part of the record.

Restricted names stay restricted.

Old routes are not reopened simply because Moonmere says they existed.

Every claim has to survive both history and the present ground.

This is slower than people in a hurry want.

Sometimes painfully slower.

But it becomes harder to pretend that a road “always worked this way” when anyone can point to the page showing otherwise.

The new map has margins wide enough for corrections.

It will need them.

## Low legitimacy

Candlemere wins the argument it was built to win.

The evidence survives.

The ledger, route claims, inspection disputes and old chart references stop being things powerful people can erase by insisting nobody remembers them correctly.

That matters.

What happens next is less certain.

An Archive can prove a crossing existed.

It cannot repair the crossing.

It can document a medicine delay.

It cannot make yesterday arrive on time.

The party contributes testimony, recovered evidence and enough local truth that the record is better than it would have been without them.

The March now knows more clearly what it has failed to do.

Whether institutions act on that knowledge becomes the next argument.

# The Many Roads

## High legitimacy

The official map still ends at the reeds.

People keep walking.

The Ashen Veil preserves side crossings, ferry arrangements, back-room messages and the practical knowledge of people who never expected a formal road to serve them.

But the settlement is not simply Nera Vale's victory.

The party is trusted widely enough to demand limits from people who built their usefulness partly on having none written down.

Predatory tolls lose protection.

Medicine routes become harder to treat as ordinary smuggling when someone wants leverage.

Witnesses exist outside the Veil.

Nera complains about all of it.

Then uses the new arrangement.

Formal authorities dislike admitting how many useful journeys happen beyond their schedules.

They also begin quietly relying on them when a flood takes a bridge.

The Many Roads remain unequal, improvised and difficult to audit.

They also remain difficult for any one institution to close.

At dawn, a ferry leaves with no public timetable and exactly the people who needed to be aboard.

## Low legitimacy

The Veil needs no regional charter to keep moving.

That is partly the point.

As official routes tighten, side paths become busier. Blacksalt brokers know which crossings remain shallow, which patrol is tired and which ferryman can be persuaded to make one more trip.

People left outside formal systems still have options.

People without contacts do not always know what those options are.

The party is known well enough to receive favours and warn against a few abuses.

It is not known widely enough to make the network answer to rules it never accepted.

The road on the official map ends at the reeds.

What happens after that depends on who knows whom.

# The Common Road

## High legitimacy

The agreement at Watcher's Rest takes three pages, four marks and most of the afternoon.

Nobody leaves satisfied.

That may be why it survives the week.

The Gilt Caravan Guild commits freight and repair crews to priority routes, but cannot decide priority alone.

The Wardens keep emergency powers, but closure orders have limits and records.

The Archive publishes obligations and disputed claims, but does not get to treat documentation as ownership.

Unofficial routes remain tolerated when they are carrying genuine need rather than predation.

Every clause contains the seed of a future argument.

Oswin says coordination will be slower.

Edda says the limits will cost time during the wrong emergency.

Holt dislikes tolerating routes he cannot fully document.

Nera dislikes giving official people a vocabulary for things they used to have to ask about.

They sign anyway.

The party does not solve scarcity.

It forces four institutions to admit that none of them is entitled to pretend the cost belongs somewhere else.

A week later, somebody has already written an angry correction beneath the public repair notice.

The road is still open.

Nobody owns it.

Everybody has work to do.

## Low legitimacy

There is no Common Road in the grand sense.

There is, however, a smaller agreement.

A ferry stays open under shared cost.

A Warden closure receives an end date.

An Archive copy is made public.

A Veil runner carries medicine without being searched at one specific checkpoint.

The party cannot bind the Grey March into a single compact.

It can prove, in several places, that cooperation is possible.

Those examples survive.

Whether they become precedent depends on people with more authority and, perhaps, less patience.

The party leaves Watcher's Rest without a regional settlement.

Behind them, two representatives remain at the table.

They are still arguing.

That is not nothing.

# The Fractured March

## High local achievement

Hearthwick may have safer wagons.

Alderwatch may have medicine.

Saint Rhel's lamp may burn beside a returned reliquary.

Candlemere may hold evidence nobody can honestly deny.

Blacksalt may have one working truce in the reeds.

None of it becomes a shared regional settlement.

The Gilt Caravan Guild strengthens routes it can maintain.

The Wardens control routes they can patrol.

The Archive accumulates claims, maps and obligations.

The Ashen Veil expands through the gaps.

The party is remembered warmly in more than one place.

The March simply never learns to remember them together.

At Redwater Ferry, Vesk keeps four sets of instructions behind the booth and obeys whichever one is least impossible that morning.

The Grey March still has roads.

It no longer agrees where they lead.

## Low legitimacy / many unresolved threads

The eighteenth day does not arrive with a verdict.

It arrives with weather.

Routes narrow.

Institutions make decisions in rooms the party is not invited into.

Some of those decisions are sensible.

Some merely become normal before anyone has time to object.

The party leaves marks anyway.

A person helped.

A road crossed.

A promise kept or broken.

A conversation someone remembers.

Those things matter even when they do not become policy.

At a fork outside Hearthwick, a traveller checks two signs, asks which authority controls each direction, and chooses the third path.

The Grey March continues.
